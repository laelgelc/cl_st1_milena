    You—first-year seminarian, the only one with a black star on your forehead, stand at the solemn assembly, swear loyalty to pain—severe and unyielding.
    You are Celus.

    Trembling arms embrace you,
    because you—are Celus.

    For the first time you cut into living flesh—the cold of the blade in your palm, the smell of metal mingling with the bitterness of fear and responsibility.
    You are Celus.

    You stand at the secret gatherings of heretics, their voices whispering of the truth priests conceal, of faith that breaks chains.
    You are Celus.

    The hands of Dakhhar—faithful and silent—install your first implant, a pulse-stabilizer, the throbbing pain of metal under your skin.
    You are Celus.

    Agnetius’s lips slide along your cheekbone, tenderness in the midst of austerity, light in the shadow of your world.
    You are Celus.

    In secret you trace the words of a forbidden prayer in the library—letters burning and pulsing like living fire in your fingers.
    You are Celus.

    A song in an unknown tongue trembles in your bones—it frightens, and it calls, and you at once want to flee and to stay, to listen and to forget.
    You are Celus.
